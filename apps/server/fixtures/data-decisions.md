# Data for Decisions — Evidence and experiments

This is synthetic study material for Ada's exploration courses.

## Module 1: who is represented?

A sample is the set of observations collected. A target population is the group
we want to understand. A large sample can still be biased if the collection
process systematically excludes relevant people.

Imagine a campus café asking only its morning customers about opening hours.
The responses can describe those customers but may miss students who arrive
after lunch. Adding more morning responses does not fix that coverage gap.

## Module 2: compare rates, not counts

Suppose a synthetic sign-up experiment has these results:

| Variant | Visitors | Sign-ups |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| A | 100 | 20 |
| B | 200 | 30 |

Variant B has more sign-ups in absolute terms. Variant A has the higher
observed sign-up rate: 20/100 = 20%, compared with 30/200 = 15% for B.
The absolute number of successes and the proportion of successes answer
different questions. The denominators must be visible when comparing rates.

These small example counts do not, on their own, establish that either design
will perform better for the target population. A decision also needs the
experiment's assignment process, uncertainty and relevant practical costs.

## Module 3: uncertainty and scope

An observed difference is evidence, not a guarantee about the next sample.
State what was measured, over which interval, and for which population before
turning an observation into a recommendation.

If a learner correctly compares the two rates, that supports an observation
about ratio reasoning in this exercise. It does not establish that the learner
understands random assignment, sampling bias or statistical uncertainty.

## A teaching variant: equalize the denominators

Translate the café example into groups of 100 people. Ask how many successes
would occur in a group of that size at each observed rate. Then restore the
original denominators and explain why counts alone favored B. Keep this as
another route to the same concept, not a new conflicting definition.
